\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Context}

The ride-sharing problem is fundamentally about correctly connecting two groups of people. One group is the customers, who need a ride from their current position to somewhere else, and the other is the drivers, each of whom owns a vehicle that they can use to take a customer from their origin to their destination. In the past, ride-sharing mostly meant customers calling a taxi service to order a taxi to their location with a destination. Nowadays, the process is much more real-time and complex, as ride-sharing programs have integrated the Internet, mobile phones and smartphones into their services~\cite{chan2012ridesharing}. Ride-sharing platforms use algorithms to assign drivers to customers as efficiently as possible~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing}. This can mean optimizing potentially thousands of rides each minute.

The ride-sharing problem covers a larger family of problems. For example, ride-sharing can mean pooling, in which several customers share the same vehicle for part of their trips, or it can mean ride-hailing, in which each customer gets their own vehicle, and the ride-sharing platform chooses which driver transports which customers. What all these variants share is prearrangement by the platform, which separates them from hailing a taxi on the street~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing,chan2012ridesharing}. The problem can also vary in what information is available before solving the problem. For example, we may have the pickup times and origin and destination locations of all drivers immediately, or we may receive them dynamically over time, in which case the matching must be done automatically on very short notice~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing}. While the details differ in each variation, the core of the problem stays the same: how to assign a set of customer requests to a set of vehicles. This thesis investigates a more static version of the problem: we know all parameters for each customer before solving, but we solve the drivers' schedules separately.

The strategy to solve the problem requires two decisions. First, we need to assign customers to drivers in a suitable way. Customers may have preferences for the kind of driver or vehicle they would like to travel with. For example, these can include age, gender, vehicle brand, and whether the vehicle is electric. Some preferences can be deal-breakers, meaning a customer will not travel with some driver. Such screening is driven largely by trust between strangers and folding it into the matching itself is known as high-dimensional matching~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing,chaube2010trust}. Second, we need to figure out ride timing. Rides must be ordered so, for example, that each customer does not arrive too late at their destination and the driver does not have to travel too far without a customer. These two decisions have well-known mathematical problem definitions. The assignment is a matching problem, and the timing is a scheduling problem; both are combinatorial problems that are difficult to solve using known methods. While difficult individually, the decisions also constrain each other: a customer that is not matched to a driver cannot be scheduled for that driver.

The objective is to find a good-enough compromise between two groups of people with different priorities. Customers want their driver to arrive exactly when they are ready and for the ride itself to be fast, while drivers want to maximise their earnings. These interests need to be balanced in one objective function, which typically weighs the conflicting aims of serving as many customers as possible, keeping operating cost low and keeping customer inconvenience small~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing,agatz2012optimization}.

Customers wait at their pickup locations for a driver to be selected, and drivers wait for rides to be assigned. In reality, hundreds of customers may be waiting at a given moment, along with tens of drivers. Also, the number of locations is much larger in reality.

Simply put, the problem studied in this thesis is the following: each customer provides their desired pickup time, destination location, and a list of drivers they are willing to be assigned to. Each driver has a shift with a fixed start and end time. A solution consists of assigning customers to drivers and then determining the best schedule for each driver. In our problem, not all customers will be served. The solution's performance is measured by how many customers were not served, how long customers waited for pickup after their desired pickup times, and how much of the shift drivers spent idle.

\subsection{Motivation}

Despite us having all the technology needed for succesful ride-sharing, it has not grown like we would expect. Even as smartphones and real-time communication has removed obstacles between people, the percentage of work trips made by ride-sharing decreased by almost ten percent over thirty years~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing}. Ride-sharing still accounts for around 8-11 percent of all trips in the North America despite hundreds of ride-sharing platforms operating in the region~\cite{chan2012ridesharing}. The problem is no longer connection the people, but deciding who to connect with whom.

Firstly, an algorithm solving the problem quickly becomes complex, and using the algorithm can be very time-consuming and possibly expensive. A system that matches thousands of customers to hundreds of drivers faces a combinatorial optimization problem which is very constrained by the needs and wants of both groups. Also as a system performing matching must answers within minutes, possibly seconds, an exact solution cannot be calculated. A natural solution is to split the problem into multiple smaller problems that are easier to solve independently, and to accept that the solution might not be completely optimal anymore. This is a reasonable trade, and in this thesis we try an alternative way of approaching the problem.

Secondly, a solution is only useful if both the customers and drivers accept it. Trust, together with convenience and incentives, decide whether a person takes part in a ride-sharing service at all~\cite{chaube2010trust}, yet the user is usually assumed to perform the screening instead of it being built into the matching~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing}. Drivers are not treated any better as the objectives used in the literature are mostly system-wide or customer-facing, such as total vehicle-miles, total travel time or the number of customers served~\cite{furuhata2013ridesharing,agatz2012optimization}. The driver appears mainly as a cost. A model that ignores either side can produce schedules that are optimal on paper and refused in practice.

\subsection{Structure}

The rest of this thesis is organized in the following way. Section~\ref{sec:literature-review} reviews the earlier work on ride-sharing and matching problems. Section~\ref{sec:methods} presents the methodology of the thesis. It introduces the problem statement, the three-step pipeline, the maximum-flow formulation of the matching problem, the mixed-integer linear program that schedules each single driver and finally the conflict resolution step that ensures each customer is assigned to only one driver.
