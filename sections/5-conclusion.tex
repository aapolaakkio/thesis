\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this thesis, we investigated a formulation of the ride-sharing problem. We formulated a ride-sharing problem having multiple drivers as a combination of multiple separate problems that have a single driver. We looked at the problem as a combination of three subproblems; matching, scheduling and conflict resolution. We formulated a three step pipeline that first matches customers to drivers based on the preferences of the customers, then solves each driver's schedule independently of the schedules of other drivers and lastly performs greedy conflict resolution to get the final conflict-free schedules of each driver.

We found that the runtime of three steps scale linearly with the number of customers and shows no clear trend when increasing the number of drivers. The scheduling step took the least amount of time by far, taking only a bit over 10 seconds at most, while the conflict resolution took almost over 190 seconds for the scenario with 8000 customers and 100 drivers. The matching step took around 120 seconds for the largest scenarios. All in all, the largest scenarios took a bit over five minutes to solve.

While taking long to solve, the conflict resolution improved the objective value from the initial scheduling round by only 9--17\%. In the over-provisioned reference scenario with 1000 customers and 200 drivers, the conflict resolution step even worsened the result by almost 11\%. The resolve was always optimal for the driver in question, with the current assignable set of customers, but wasn't necessarily optimal for the whole driver fleet. Due to the greedy approach of the conflict resolution, the solved schedules might have not be the best possible the effect of which can be inflated in customer-dense scenarios like the reference scenario. Also, considering approximately 2/3 of the customers were unserved in all but the reference scenario, all of the used scenarios are rather customer-dense. The driver order in which the conflict resolution is performed had almost no difference in the result as the median excess over the per-scenario best ordering is below 0.11\% for all strategies. While the ascending conflicts ordering had both the smallest spread and median excess, no single ordering won consistently.

As previously said, we investigated only one formulation of the ride-sharing problem. In our mathematical model we only considered the time and coverage dimension of the problem and modeled, for example, the distance dimension with only a simple mandatory break between all rides. In reality we would need to account for the actual time used to travel between dropoff and pickup locations. Naturally, there are many more aspects which could be investigated; traffic, travel cost and road network, to name a few. The conflict resolution algorithm could also be improved to ensure a more globally optimal result. The actual conflict resolution metric could also be improved as we used only the pickup delay in our model.In addition, the matching algorithm could be improved. We used Ford-Fulkerson with Edmonds-Karp implementation but there already exists improved algorithms for solving the maximum flow through a graph.
