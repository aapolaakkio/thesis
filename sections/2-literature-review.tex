\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

The ways of solving the ride-sharing problem as well as matching and scheduling alone have been studied extensively, especially over the last 20 years. The past literature approaches the ride-sharing problem from both operations research and systems perspectives. This section reviews the history of the ride-sharing problem, how it has been formulated and solved before together while also reviewing the history of matching and scheduling in literature. 

The operations research view defines the problem as a routing problem and places it in the category of pickup-and-delivery problems, in which vehicles transport loads from origins to destinations~\citep{savelsbergh1995general}. The dial-a-ride problem is the special case where the loads are people~\citep{savelsbergh1995general}, ride-sharing is similar to its dynamic variant~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}, and the customer experience is included in the objective~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} gives an exact dynamic programming algorithm for a single-vehicle many-to-many problem that minimizes a weighted combination of total service time and customer dissatisfaction. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} also shows that the computational effort grows exponentially in the size of the problem. Exact algorithms remain limited to small instances, while heuristics are the only methods that reach large ones~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. Also, in the dynamic case, where requests are revealed over time, a solution can no longer be a fixed plan but a strategy that reacts to new information~\citep{berbeglia2010dynamic}. The matching part of the problem has an even longer history as an assignment problem~\citep{pentico2007assignment}. Quite recently, \citet{masoud2017realtime} match each customer one at a time against all drivers, allowing riders to transfer between vehicles, and solve this many-to-one problem optimally with a dynamic program over a time-expanded network.

The literature viewing the problem for a systems view investigates and builds actual matching programs to solve the problem. \citet{hall1997dynamic} studied dynamic ride-sharing, both analytically and through an implementation in Los Angeles, and found that a busy road produces a large enough pool of potential matches in theory. However, in practice a customer has, at best, in an emergency, a one-in-five chance per person contacted of being offered a ride. \citet{dailey1999seattle} matched riders through a series of web page forms, ran the system beside a traditional regional program for a year and reached about the same number of new users as the traditional regional program over nine months. The Los Angeles experiment shows that a match feasible in theory is not necessarily a match people accept~\citep{hall1997dynamic}. \citet{agatz2012optimization} survey the optimization side of the dynamic problem and conclude that centralized matching may be computationally infeasible for large cities, so a decomposition approach is necessary. This thesis investigates the problem by decomposing the problem into matching, per-driver scheduling, and conflict resolution.

\subsection{Ride-sharing}

The term "ride-sharing" is not defined in a single way in the literature. The broadest definition, by \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing}, defines it as a joint trip with more than one participant sharing a vehicle. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} also require participants to coordinate where and when each person is picked up and dropped off. \citet{chan2012ridesharing} add that the driver shares the same origin or destination as the passengers and that the driver does not financially benefit from the arrangement. \citet{agatz2012optimization} give a similar definition but also requires that drivers are "independent private entities" and that trips are "single, non-recurring". \citet{mitropoulos2021systematic} find that, in the literature, the term is used for "both profit and non-profit ridesharing services". \citet{pigalle2023ridesharing} separate ride-sharing from ride-hailing, which refers to the for-profit connection between riders and drivers. The organized variants share prearrangement by a service provider, which separates ride-sharing from hailing a taxi on the street~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}.

The literature is also divided by who runs the ride-sharing service. \citet{chan2012ridesharing} classify ride-sharing based on the relationship between people involved, separating acquaintance-based arrangements from organization-based ones where participating people have a membership or join through a website. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} make a similar distinction on the provider side, distinguishing matching agencies that only introduce independent drivers and customers to each other, and service operators that have their own vehicles and drivers. These two cases have different problems. For an agency, both participating sides have to screen the other side themselves, which makes trust a precondition for matching~\citep{chaube2010trust}. For an operator, most of the decisions are made by them, while the customer only needs to decide whether to take part~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}. This gives the fixed-fleet problem familiar from operations research, where vehicles have capacities and fixed start and end locations~\citep{savelsbergh1995general} and requests can be turned down when the drivers cannot serve them~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. The scenario studied in this thesis falls into the operator category, where drivers work fixed shifts.

\subsection{Matching}

Matching customers to drivers is, in its most general form, an assignment problem, a problem family which has a long history and many variants and algorithms~\citep{pentico2007assignment}. A classic assignment problem makes one-to-one assignments so that the total cost is minimized, a problem that is efficiently solvable~\citep{pentico2007assignment, edmonds1972theoretical}. Ride-sharing is different from this in a couple of aspects. The assignment is usually not a one-to-one matching, as a driver most likely serves several customers during a shift~\citep{savelsbergh1995general} and, when a matching agency does the matching, a customer is offered more than one driver to choose from~\citep{dailey1999seattle}. Also, the cost of an individual match is not known before actual assignment since it depends on the other matches the driver has. In the dial-a-ride problem, the feasibility of adding a request is a function of the vehicle's current route~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. The objective is then the number of matches rather than their total cost~\citep{agatz2012optimization}. A capacitated matching like this is more natural to express as a flow problem, where the capacities of the customers and drivers become arc capacities and the value of a maximum flow equals the capacity of a minimum cut~\citep{ford1956maximal}. The maximum flow can be calculated by augmenting paths that have residual capacity left, and selecting those by a breadth-first search limits the number of augmentations independently of the capacities~\citep{edmonds1972theoretical}. Since the capacities here are integral, a maximum flow of such is integral as well~\citep{edmonds1972theoretical}, so the flow on the customer–driver arcs can be read as an assignment, the classic assignment problem being equivalent to a weighted bipartite matching~\citep{pentico2007assignment}.

Matching is an integral part of ride-sharing, and \citet{agatz2012optimization} argue that solving it for a large city may not be computationally feasible, meaning it must be decomposed. \citet{masoud2017realtime}, on the other hand, keep time feasibility inside the matching, and achieve this by matching a customer only once there is a feasible schedule for the ride. Exact methods for the dial-a-ride problem scale poorly, as noted above~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. In comparison, matching on a preference graph alone is cheap, and it reduces the candidate set before any matching decision is made. The performer of the matching also matters as when a service operator does the matching, customer preferences act as a feasibility constraint. A matching agency only introduces the counterparts and leaves the decision to them~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}. The preferences follow from the relationship between participants~\citep{chan2012ridesharing} and the trust between the two sides~\citep{chaube2010trust}. Preferences like here cause the graph to be sparse, causing a maximum matching on a preference basis to leave customers unserved~\citep{agatz2012optimization, masoud2017realtime}. This is consistent with the earlier observations that people don't always accept a match based on theory~\citep{hall1997dynamic}.

\subsection{Contribution}

In thesis we add the preferences of the customers as hard constraints to the matching instead of depending on the user to perform the screening for their driver. The preferences of the customers form a bipartite graph that is used to perform the matching suitable drivers for each customer. In practice, the screening is done by manual selection from stored participant profiles~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}, whether a match is accepted depends on trust between the parties~\citep{chaube2010trust}, and restrictions are treated as something that makes the matching harder~\citep{agatz2012optimization}. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} identify integrating screening into the ride-matching itself, which they call high-dimensional matching, as an open challenge, and note that screening items treated as hard constraints are the simple case. This thesis takes that simple case: each customer's list of acceptable drivers is a hard constraint on the matching.

The past literature also rarely takes the drivers into account when forming the objective of the models. The drivers usually only appear as the cost of wages~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. We instead add the driver idle time as a term in the objective and try to minimize it. Arguably, the experience of the driver should be taken into account which is what we investigate here.
