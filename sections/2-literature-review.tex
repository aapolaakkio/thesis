\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

The motivation of this thesis comes from the fact that solving the matching and the scheduling together can be expensive and time-consuming, and a solution is not good if either the customer or the driver doesn't accept it. This section reviews the history of the ride-sharing problem, how the problem has been formulated and solved before, and also what is known about the people and their behaviour that the models try to capture.

The problem of ride-sharing has been studied extensively especially during the last 20 years. It has been approached from both the directions of operations research and also from a systems direction. The operations research view defines the problem as a routing problem, and includes it in the category of pickup and delivery problems, in which vehicles transport load from some origins to some destinations~\citep{savelsbergh1995general}. Ride-sharing and the dial-a-ride problem is that family's passenger-transporting special case, where the experience of the traveller is included in the objective~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} gives an exact dynamic programming algorithm for a single vehicle many-to-many problem that minimizes a weighted combination of the total service time and the dissatisfaction of the customers. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} also shows that the computational effort grows exponentially in the number of customers. Later surveys report that exact algorithms remain restricted to small instances and that heuristics perform better as the instance size grows~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}, and that in the dynamic case, where requests are revealed over time, a solution can no longer be a fixed plan but a strategy that reacts to new information~\citep{berbeglia2010dynamic}. The matching part of the problem has an even longer history as an assignment problem which has a wide family of variants and algorithms~\citep{pentico2007assignment}.

The systems directions builds and inspects actual matching programs. \citet{hall1997dynamic} studied dynamic ride-sharing both
analytically and through an implementation in Los Angeles, and found that a busy road produces a large enough pool of potential matches in theory,
but in practice a traveller has at best, in an emergency situation, a one in five chance for a compatible person to offer a ride. \citet{dailey1999seattle} matched riders through a series of web page forms, ran the system beside a traditional regional program for a year and reached the same size userbase that the traditional regional program. Both approaches show that a match that is feasible in theory is not necessarily a match that is accepted. \citet{agatz2012optimization} survey the optimization side of the dynamic problem and conclude that centralized matching may be computationally infeasible for large cities, meaning a decomposition approach is necessary. This thesis investigates that and decomposes the problem into matching, per-driver scheduling and conflict resolution.

\subsection{Ride-sharing}

The term "ride-sharing" is not defined in a one single way in the literature. The strictest definition for it by \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} defines it as a joint trip of more than one participants who share a vehicle. The definition by \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} also requires the participants to coordinate where and when each one of them is picked up and dropped off, and the driver doesn't financially benefit from the arrangement. \citet{chan2012ridesharing} adds to the previous definition that the driver has the same origin or destination with the passengers. \citet{agatz2012optimization} gives a similar definition but requires also that the drivers are "independent private entities", and that the trips are "single and non-recurring". \citet{mitropoulos2021systematic} find that, in practice, the term is used for "both profit and non-profit ridesharing services". \citet{pigalle2023ridesharing} separates ride-sharing from ride-hailing, which means for-profit connection of riders to drivers. What the organized variants share is prearrangement by a service provider, which separates ride-sharing from hailing a taxi on the street~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}.
