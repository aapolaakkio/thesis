\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

The ways of solving the ride-sharing problem as well as matching and scheduling alone have been studied extensively, especially over the last 20 years. The past literature approaches the ride-sharing problem from both operations research and systems perspectives. This section reviews the history of the ride-sharing problem, how it has been formulated and solved before together while also reviewing the history of matching and scheduling in literature. 

The operations research view defines the problem as a routing problem and places it in the category of pickup-and-delivery problems, in which vehicles transport loads from origins to destinations~\citep{savelsbergh1995general}. Ride-sharing and the dial-a-ride problem are family-transport special cases, where the customer experience is included in the objective~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} gives an exact dynamic programming algorithm for a single-vehicle many-to-many problem that minimizes a weighted combination of total service time and customer dissatisfaction. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} also shows that the computational effort grows exponentially in the number of customers. Recent work shows that exact algorithms remain limited to small instances, while heuristics perform better as instance size grows~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. Also, in the dynamic case, where requests are revealed over time, a solution can no longer be a fixed plan but a strategy that reacts to new information~\citep{berbeglia2010dynamic}. The matching part of the problem has an even longer history as an assignment problem, which has a wide family of variants and algorithms~\citep{pentico2007assignment}.

The literature viewing the problem for a systems view investigates and builds actual matching programs to solve the problem. \citet{hall1997dynamic} studied dynamic ride-sharing, both analytically and through an implementation in Los Angeles, and found that a busy road produces a large enough pool of potential matches in theory. However, in practice a customer has, at best, in an emergency, a one-in-five chance of finding a compatible person to offer a ride. \citet{dailey1999seattle} matched riders through a series of web page forms, ran the system beside a traditional regional program for a year and reached the same size user base as the traditional regional program. Both approaches show that a match feasible in theory is not necessarily a match people accept. \citet{agatz2012optimization} survey the optimization side of the dynamic problem and conclude that centralized matching may be computationally infeasible for large cities, so a decomposition approach is necessary. This thesis investigates the problem by decomposing the problem into matching, per-driver scheduling, and conflict resolution.

\subsection{Ride-sharing}

The term "ride-sharing" is not defined in a one single way in the literature. The strictest definition, by \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing}, defines it as a joint trip with more than one participant sharing a vehicle. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} also requires participants to coordinate where and when each person is picked up and dropped off, and that the driver does not financially benefit from the arrangement. \citet{chan2012ridesharing} adds that the driver shares the same origin or destination as the passengers. \citet{agatz2012optimization} gives a similar definition but also requires that drivers are "independent private entities" and that trips are "single, non-recurring". \citet{mitropoulos2021systematic} find that, in practice, the term is used for "both profit and non-profit ride-sharing services". \citet{pigalle2023ridesharing} separates ride-sharing from ride-hailing, which refers to the for-profit connection between riders and drivers. The organized variants share prearrangement by a service provider, which separates ride-sharing from hailing a taxi on the street~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}.
