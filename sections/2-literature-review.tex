\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

The ways of solving the ride-sharing problem as well as matching and scheduling alone have been studied extensively, especially over the last 20 years. The past literature approaches the ride-sharing problem from both operations research and systems perspectives. This section reviews the history of the ride-sharing problem, how it has been formulated and solved before while also reviewing the history of matching and scheduling in literature. 

The operations research view defines the problem as a routing problem and places it in the category of pickup-and-delivery problems, in which vehicles transport loads from origins to destinations~\citep{savelsbergh1995general}. The dial-a-ride problem is the special case where the loads are people~\citep{savelsbergh1995general}, ride-sharing is similar to its dynamic variant~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}, and the customer experience is included in the objective~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} gives an exact dynamic programming algorithm for a single-vehicle many-to-many problem that minimizes a weighted combination of total service time and customer dissatisfaction. \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} also shows that the computational effort grows exponentially in the size of the problem. Exact algorithms remain limited to small instances, while heuristics are the only methods that reach large ones~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. Also, in the dynamic case, where requests are revealed over time, a solution can no longer be a fixed plan but a strategy that reacts to new information~\citep{berbeglia2010dynamic}. The matching part of the problem has an even longer history as an assignment problem~\citep{pentico2007assignment}. Quite recently, \citet{masoud2017realtime} match each customer one at a time against all drivers, allowing riders to transfer between vehicles, and solve this many-to-one problem optimally with a dynamic program over a time-expanded network.

The literature viewing the problem from a systems view investigates and builds actual matching programs to solve the problem. \citet{hall1997dynamic} studied dynamic ride-sharing, both analytically and through an implementation in Los Angeles, and found that a busy road produces a large enough pool of potential matches in theory. In practice, however, a customer had at best a one-in-five chance per person contacted of being offered a ride, even in an emergency, so a match that is feasible in theory is not necessarily a match people accept~\citep{hall1997dynamic}. \citet{dailey1999seattle} moved the matching to a series of web page forms, ran the system beside a traditional phone-based program for a year and reached about the same number of new users, which suggests that the medium of matching is not what limits participation. Their model of the system makes the number of matches proportional to the probability that two trips are compatible, so any restriction placed on a match lowers the number of matches formed~\citep{dailey1999seattle}. \citet{agatz2012optimization} survey the optimization side of the dynamic problem and conclude that centralized matching may be computationally infeasible for large cities, so a decomposition approach is necessary.

\subsection{Ride-sharing}

The term "ride-sharing" is not defined in a single way in the literature. The broadest definition, by \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing}, defines it as a joint trip with more than one participant sharing a vehicle. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} also require participants to coordinate where and when each person is picked up and dropped off. \citet{chan2012ridesharing} add that the driver shares the same origin or destination as the passengers and that the driver does not financially benefit from the arrangement. \citet{agatz2012optimization} give a similar definition but also requires that drivers are "independent private entities" and that trips are "single, non-recurring". \citet{mitropoulos2021systematic} find that, in the literature, the term is used for "both profit and non-profit ridesharing services". \citet{pigalle2023ridesharing} separate ride-sharing from ride-hailing, which refers to the for-profit connection between riders and drivers. The organized variants share prearrangement by a service provider, which separates ride-sharing from hailing a taxi on the street~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}.

The literature is also divided by who runs the ride-sharing service. \citet{chan2012ridesharing} classify ride-sharing based on the relationship between people involved, separating acquaintance-based arrangements from organization-based ones where participating people have a membership or join through a website. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} make a similar distinction on the provider side, distinguishing matching agencies that only introduce independent drivers and customers to each other, and service operators that have their own vehicles and drivers. These two cases have different problems. For an agency, both participating sides have to screen the other side themselves, which makes trust a precondition for matching~\citep{chaube2010trust}. For an operator, most of the decisions are made by them, while the customer only needs to decide whether to take part~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}. This gives the fixed-fleet problem familiar from operations research, where vehicles have capacities and fixed start and end locations~\citep{savelsbergh1995general} and requests can be turned down when the drivers cannot serve them~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. The scenario studied in this thesis falls into the operator category, where drivers work fixed shifts.

\subsection{Matching}

Matching customers to drivers is, in its most general form, an assignment problem, a problem family which has a long history and many variants and algorithms~\citep{pentico2007assignment}. A classic assignment problem makes one-to-one assignments so that the total cost is minimized, a problem that is efficiently solvable~\citep{pentico2007assignment, edmonds1972theoretical}. Ride-sharing is different from this in a couple of aspects. The assignment is usually not a one-to-one matching, as a driver most likely serves several customers during a shift~\citep{savelsbergh1995general} and, when a matching agency does the matching, a customer is offered more than one driver to choose from~\citep{dailey1999seattle}. Also, the cost of an individual match is not known before actual assignment since it depends on the other matches the driver has. In the dial-a-ride problem, the feasibility of adding a request is a function of the vehicle's current route~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. The objective is then the number of matches rather than their total cost~\citep{agatz2012optimization}. Such a capacitated bipartite matching is naturally expressed as a maximum flow problem, in which the number of drivers a customer may be offered and the number of customers a driver may serve become arc capacities~\citep{ford1957simple}, and it is solvable in polynomial time with an integral solution~\citep{edmonds1972theoretical}.

Matching is an integral part of ride-sharing, and \citet{agatz2012optimization} argue that solving it for a large city may not be computationally feasible, meaning it must be decomposed. \citet{masoud2017realtime}, on the other hand, keep time feasibility inside the matching, and achieve this by matching a customer only once there is a feasible schedule for the ride. Exact methods for the dial-a-ride problem scale poorly, as noted above~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. The performer of the matching also matters as when a service operator does the matching, customer preferences act as a feasibility constraint. A matching agency only introduces the counterparts and leaves the decision to them~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}. The preferences follow from the relationship between participants~\citep{chan2012ridesharing} and the trust between the two sides~\citep{chaube2010trust}. Preferences like here cause the graph to be sparse, causing a maximum matching on a preference basis to leave customers unserved~\citep{agatz2012optimization, masoud2017realtime}. This is consistent with the earlier observations that people don't always accept a match based on theory~\citep{hall1997dynamic}.

\subsection{Scheduling}

Choosing when each ride of a driver takes place is a scheduling problem in the classical sense. In machine scheduling, each job has a processing time, a weight, a release date on which it becomes available and a due date, and each machine can handle at most one job at a time~\citep{lenstra1977complexity}. In the these terms a driver is a single machine, a ride is a job whose processing time is the ride duration, and the wanted pickup time is the release date. The optimality criteria are functions of the completion times of the jobs, such as the maximum lateness, the total tardiness or the number of late jobs~\citep{lenstra1977complexity}. A late pickup is then a tardiness and an unserved request plays the role of a late job. Release dates alone make a single machine problem hard. \citet{lenstra1977complexity} show that a non-zero release date for a single job is already enough to make minimizing the maximum lateness, the total completion time or the number of late jobs NP-complete. Therefore, exact solutions rely on integer programming. \citet{dyer1990formulating} compare several formulations of the single machine problem with release dates and conclude that the formulations based on time discretisation give the strongest lower bounds. In their strongest formulation a binary variable equals one if the processing of a job starts at a given time, which makes the feasible set essentially independent of the data but gives the formulation a very large number of variables~\citep{dyer1990formulating}. What the classical criteria do not measure is the machine, since all of them are functions of the job completion times~\citep{lenstra1977complexity}. In the dial-a-ride problem the waiting time of the vehicle at a stop is a variable of the scheduling problem and some variants bound it from above, but the driver still enters the objective only through wages~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}.

In the dial-a-ride literature, scheduling is the step that follows routing. Given a route, the scheduling problem should determine the departure time from the depot and the time at which the ride begins at each stop so that the time windows are satisfied and the route duration is minimized~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. For a fixed route this is easy, as the optimal departure time can be determined in time linear in the length of the route~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. The difficulty comes from choosing the route, as for a single vehicle the exact dynamic program of \citet{psaraftis1980dynamic} grows exponentially in the number of requests. Multi-vehicle methods therefore decompose the problem. A commonly used technique defines clusters of users to be served by the same vehicle before the routing phase and then applies a single-vehicle algorithm to each cluster, and some methods alternate between a routing step and a scheduling step~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. Once the customers of a vehicle are fixed and each ride is treated as a given block of time, what remains for the vehicle is the single machine problem described above. The clusters are not independent, however, and the early cluster-first heuristics already make swaps between the clusters after solving each of them~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. \citet{agatz2012optimization} expect a decomposition to be necessary for ride-sharing in large cities as well, but it should be noted that a geographic partition is problematic since the origin and the destination of a ride may fall in different subregions. \citet{masoud2017realtime} take the opposite route and keep time feasibility inside the matching, constructing a time-expanded feasible network for one rider at a time and searching it with a dynamic program, at the cost of processing the riders one after another.

\subsection{Contribution}

This thesis investigates the problem by decomposing it into matching, per-driver scheduling, and conflict resolution, following the decomposition that \citet{agatz2012optimization} expect to be necessary for large cities. Matching on the preference graph alone is cheap, as a maximum flow is solvable in polynomial time~\citep{edmonds1972theoretical}, and it reduces the candidate set of each driver before scheduling. The conflict resolution step plays the role of the swaps between clusters that the cluster-first heuristics make after solving each cluster~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}.

We add the preferences of the customers as hard constraints to the matching instead of depending on the user to perform the screening for their driver. The preferences of the customers form a bipartite graph on which suitable drivers are matched to each customer. In practice, the screening is done by manual selection from stored participant profiles~\citep{furuhata2013ridesharing}, whether a match is accepted depends on trust between the parties~\citep{chaube2010trust}, and restrictions are treated as something that makes the matching harder~\citep{agatz2012optimization}. \citet{furuhata2013ridesharing} identify integrating screening into the ride-matching itself, which they call high-dimensional matching, as an open challenge, and note that screening items treated as hard constraints are the simple case. This thesis takes that simple case: each customer's list of acceptable drivers is a hard constraint on the matching.

The past literature also rarely takes the drivers into account when forming the objective of the models. The drivers usually only appear as the cost of wages~\citep{cordeau2007dialaride}. We instead add the driver idle time as a term in the objective and try to minimize it. Arguably, the experience of the driver should be taken into account which is what we investigate here.
